Excel or Power BI: when it is worth making the jump

Excel is still a great tool, but it has limits. Six signs your company needs to move to Power BI and how to make the transition.

The DeepDatas team · July 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Excel is probably the most used analysis tool in the world, and for good reason: it is flexible, everyone knows how to use it and it solves a huge number of problems fast. There is no need to fight Excel. But as a company grows, it starts asking Excel for things it was never designed to do.

Power BI, Microsoft's dashboard tool, then shows up as the natural next step. When is it worth taking? These are the signs we see most often.

Six signs the moment has come

1. Building the report takes days

If every month-end someone spends a day or more copying data from different systems, pasting it into a spreadsheet and checking formulas, the problem is neither the person nor the spreadsheet: it is the process. In Power BI, loading and transforming data is defined once and repeats on its own.

2. There are several versions of the same number

"Sales_final.xlsx", "Sales_final_v2.xlsx", "Sales_final_reviewed.xlsx". When every department has its own copy, the numbers stop matching and meetings go on arguing about which one is right. A central data model in Power BI means everyone queries the same source.

3. Spreadsheets get slow or unstable

Files with hundreds of thousands of rows, lookups that take minutes to recalculate, workbooks that close by themselves. Power BI is built to work with millions of records and stay responsive.

4. The data comes from several systems

When the analysis needs the ERP crossed with the CRM, the targets spreadsheet and the distributors' files, Excel forces you to do that cross-referencing by hand every time. Power BI connects to each source and relates them in one model.

5. You need to control who sees what

Emailing a spreadsheet means losing control: it gets forwarded, copied, modified. In Power BI you define permissions per user — so each rep sees only their own book, for example — and you can audit who accessed what.

6. Decisions arrive late

If the report is ready on the 10th of the following month, problems get spotted when it is already too late to fix them. A dashboard that refreshes itself, every day, lets you act in time.

Excel and Power BI are not competitors

Making the jump does not mean abandoning spreadsheets. In practice they live together very well:

  • Excel is still ideal for one-off analysis, simulations, budgets and manual inputs such as sales targets.
  • Power BI takes over as the official source of the indicators people check all the time, and as the meeting point for data from different systems.

You can even analyse the Power BI model's data from Excel, with pivot tables, for people who prefer to keep working in spreadsheets.

How to make the transition

A common mistake is trying to move every report to Power BI at once. Going in stages works better:

  1. Pick the most painful report: the one that eats the most hours or causes the most arguments.
  2. Review the source data before designing the dashboard: most of a report's problems are in the data, not in the tool.
  3. Automate the load from each source, so the dashboard refreshes itself.
  4. Design the dashboard with the people who will use it, starting from the questions they need answered.
  5. Train the team and retire the Excel version once the dashboard has been validated.

With the first dashboard running, the rest of the reports follow much faster, because most of the data model is already built.

In short

If you recognized your company in two or more of these signs, it has probably passed the point where Excel is enough. The good news is that you do not need a huge project to start: one well-built dashboard already changes how people work.
