A good sales dashboard is not the one with the most charts, but the one that answers the questions that matter, fast: are we going to hit the target? where are we losing sales? which customer needs attention?

These are the eight indicators we recommend tracking week by week, how they are calculated and what decisions they support.

## 1. Sales against target

**Calculation:** cumulative sales for the period ÷ target for the period.

The most basic indicator and the most important one. It pays to see it broken down by rep, territory, channel and category, and compared against expected progress: hitting 50% of target halfway through the month is good news; hitting 50% on the 25th is not.

## 2. Year-on-year growth

**Calculation:** sales for the period ÷ sales for the same period last year − 1.

Comparing against the same moment last year neutralizes seasonality. Where there is inflation it pays to measure it in units or at constant prices too, to tell real growth from price increases.

## 3. Active customers

**Calculation:** number of customers who bought at least once in the period.

A fall in active customers usually comes before a fall in sales. Tracking it alongside sales helps you tell whether the business is growing because customers are being added or because the same ones are buying more.

## 4. Customers at risk

**Calculation:** customers who used to buy regularly and have not bought for longer than usual.

One of the most actionable indicators: it produces a concrete list of customers the sales team should call this week, before losing them.

## 5. Average order value

**Calculation:** sales for the period ÷ number of orders or invoices.

It helps you understand whether sales moved because of the number of orders or the size of each one, and to measure the effect of promotions or price lists.

## 6. Coverage of the customer book

**Calculation:** points of sale buying a product line ÷ active points of sale.

It shows how much of each customer's potential is being used. Low coverage on a line is a cross-selling opportunity: customers who already buy from you, but not everything they could.

## 7. Gross margin

**Calculation:** (sales − cost of goods sold) ÷ sales.

Selling more is not always better if you sell at a thinner margin. Seeing it by customer, product and channel helps you spot excessive discounts or products worth reviewing.

## 8. Days sales outstanding

**Calculation:** accounts receivable balance ÷ average daily sales.

It closes the commercial loop: a sale you do not collect is not a good sale. A rise in days outstanding can be an early warning of financial trouble at some customers.

## How to put these indicators into practice

Having the list is easy; the hard part is that the numbers are available, up to date and reliable. A few recommendations:

- **One single source per indicator.** If sales and finance calculate margin differently, the indicator loses its value.
- **Written definitions.** What counts as an "active customer"? Do credit notes reduce sales? Agreeing once saves endless arguments.
- **Automatic refresh.** A dashboard that depends on someone updating it by hand ends up out of date.
- **Few indicators, well chosen.** A dashboard with eight indicators people look at beats one with forty nobody understands.
- **From indicator to action.** Each indicator should let you drill down: from "the north territory is below target" to "these five customers stopped buying".

If pulling these numbers together takes days today, the first step is not designing the dashboard: it is [getting the data that feeds it in order](/en/blog/data-quality/).
